What an Elliott Wave trader needs to know

1 · Start from the top
  1. Count the weekly and daily first. They set the degree and the main trend.
  2. Then drop to 4H and 1H to see where you are inside that bigger wave.
  3. A lower-timeframe count that fights the bigger degree is a correction until proven otherwise.
  4. Always say which degree you mean. "Wave 3" on 15m is a ripple inside a daily wave.
2 · Three rules, no exceptions
  1. Wave 2 never goes past the start of wave 1.
  2. Wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5.
  3. Wave 4 never enters wave 1's price territory (diagonals excepted).

Break one and the count is wrong. That price is the count's invalidation level, which is why it is drawn in red.

3 · Guidelines are tendencies
  • Wave 2 often retraces 50–61.8% of wave 1. Wave 3 is often about 1.618× wave 1 and carries the most volume.
  • Wave 4 often retraces about 38.2% of wave 3. Wave 5 is often about the size of wave 1.
  • Alternation: a sharp wave 2 tends to come with a sideways wave 4, and the other way round.
  • In crypto, one motive wave usually extends, often the 3rd, and 5th-wave blow-offs happen in euphoria.

An academic test found Fibonacci ratios in the Dow no more often than chance (Batchelor & Ramyar, 2006). Treat fib levels as zones that need other confluence.

4 · Corrective patterns
  • Zigzag 5-3-5: sharp; C goes beyond the end of A.
  • Flat 3-3-5: sideways; B retraces ~90%+ of A. In an expanded flat B goes past A's start, a classic stop-hunt shape.
  • Triangle 3-3-3-3-3: in wave 4 or B, just before the last move.
  • Combination W-X-Y: two or three simple corrections joined.
  • Diagonal: a wedge where wave 4 may overlap wave 1. An ending diagonal in wave 5 or C signals exhaustion.
5 · Always carry an alternate
  • A count with no invalidation level is a story, not analysis.
  • Pros publish a primary count, an alternate count and the exact price that kills the primary.
  • If two reasonable counts point opposite ways, the honest read is "no edge from Elliott here".
  • Never relabel the waves after a rule breaks to keep an idea alive. Recount from a clean sheet.
6 · Where the structure is clearest
  • After a completed wave 2 or B: the rule level is close and the next wave (3 or C) is usually the largest, so the structure is easiest to judge.
  • Wave 5: the weakest place to start. It is the shortest remaining move and the most likely to fail or truncate.
  • 5th waves with RSI divergence or an ending-diagonal shape point to exhaustion. They are not a reversal by themselves; wait for structure to confirm.
7 · Add confluence
  • Volume: wave 3 should carry the heaviest volume; a 5th on falling volume is tired.
  • Wyckoff: a spring / LPS near a wave-2 low, or a UTAD / LPSY near a 5th-wave high, tells the same story from a different angle.
  • BTC first: an alt's wave count rarely survives a BTC count pointing the other way.
  • Positioning: funding, open interest and liquidation clusters show who is trapped at a wave end.
8 · Mistakes that cost the most
  • Calling the top of a 3rd wave. This was the map's own main error in the replay.
  • Forcing a count on a choppy, illiquid coin. If no count fits cleanly, there is no count.
  • Counting on one timeframe only.
  • Moving the invalidation level when price reaches it.
  • Treating fib ratios as exact targets instead of zones.
9 · Risk comes first
  • Elliott Wave is subjective. Analysts often disagree on the same chart, and published counts get revised.
  • The pros in our check were right on direction 7 of 8 times, and one count still failed in a single crash day.
  • Experienced traders decide their maximum loss before anything else and keep it small. A wave count never replaces that.
